# Artist's Manifesto

**GitHub Repository:** [https://github.com/smhavci/KNOCK](https://github.com/smhavci/KNOCK)

## *The Door I Didn't Know I Was Leaving*

### A Statement on KNOCK

---

There is a particular kind of loss that has no name. Not grief, not regret — something quieter and more disorienting. It is the loss you discover only in retrospect: the last time you did something as a child before you stopped being one. The last time you played without a concept of playing. The last afternoon you fell asleep in the back seat of a car and trusted completely that someone else would carry you inside.

You didn't know it was the last time. That's the whole thing. If you had known, you would have paid attention differently. But you didn't, and so the moment passed unmarked, and somewhere without your noticing a door swung shut behind you.

This project is about that door.

---

## Why This Song

In 1973, Bob Dylan wrote "Knockin' on Heaven's Door" for a film. Not a great occasion — a dying sheriff in a Sam Peckinpah western, taking off his badge because he can't use it anymore. Dylan wrote it in an afternoon. It was supposed to be small, functional, scored for a three-minute scene.

It became one of the most covered songs in history. Not because of the melody, and not because of any lyrical complexity — the song has maybe forty words. It became universal because of one image: the laying down. The badge. The thing you carried until it stopped being yours.

Everyone knows that feeling. Not death — Dylan is not really writing about death. He is writing about the moment something you were, something you held, quietly becomes past tense. The moment a role you played, a self you inhabited, a season you lived inside, stops fitting. You put it down. You knock on a different door. You don't know yet what's on the other side.

I had been thinking about this for years before I made this project. I was thinking about it because I was — like most people my age — trying to understand what had happened to my childhood. Not in a therapeutic way. Just in the way that you sometimes stop and wonder: when exactly did that end? What was the last day of it? Was there a last day, or does it just thin out until it's gone?

There was no last day I could name. That's what bothered me. And that's where KNOCK came from.

## What I Made

KNOCK is a web application. You type a childhood memory — a specific one, a small one, the kind you might not have thought about in years. The system responds with two things: a poem, and a photograph.

The poem is written by Claude, Anthropic's large language model, inside a very specific set of constraints. I wrote the system prompt over several days, trying to get it right. I gave Claude Dylan's 1973 voice: spare, plain-spoken, no decoration, no abstractions. I gave it craft rules I believe in — use only concrete images, never explain an emotion, end on an image not a conclusion. I gave it prohibitions: no sentimentality, no rhyme unless it arrives naturally, no naming the feeling, trust the image to carry it.

The photograph is generated by DALL-E 3, but it doesn't illustrate the memory directly. First, a second, faster model extracts the *visual core* of the poem — the one image that holds the emotional weight. The one photograph a stranger would feel without knowing the story. Then that scene is rendered as a 35mm film photograph from 1973: heavy grain, faded amber and sepia, available light, Sam Peckinpah stillness. No faces. No modern elements. No digital sharpness.

After you generate your door, it joins a collective gallery. An anonymous wall of other people's lost childhoods. You can scroll through it. You can read strangers' poems. Sometimes you read someone else's memory and you recognize it completely — not because it happened to you, but because you were there too, somewhere, in some version of it. That recognition is part of the work.

## What I Was Trying to Find

I want to be careful here, because I think it's easy to make AI-assisted art sound grander than it is. I am not claiming that I and Claude wrote these poems together as equal collaborators. I am not claiming that the images are photographs of real things that were lost. I am not making a metaphysical argument about machine creativity.

What I am claiming is this: the prompt engineering involved in this project is a form of artistic direction, and the outputs — the poems, the photographs — are genuine aesthetic objects that carry something true.

When I read the poems KNOCK produces, they are not what I would have written. They are better than what I would have written, in certain ways, and stranger. They find concrete images I would not have found. They lay lines down with a kind of earned quietness that is hard to fake. The constraint system I built — the prohibitions, the tonal markers, the craft rules — channels the model toward something I recognize as real.

The photographs are more unsettling. I didn't expect them to affect me the way they do. There is something about the grain and the light and the emptiness that makes them feel like they were actually taken in 1973, by someone who is no longer alive, of a moment that cannot be recovered. They feel archival. They feel like evidence of something.

I think what I was trying to find — and what this project tries to give back — is a form for grief that has no obvious occasion. We have rituals for death, for divorce, for losing a job. We don't have rituals for the end of childhood. We don't have a ceremony for the last time you played unselfconsciously, or the last summer that felt infinite, or the moment adulthood became the main event and childhood became the backstory.

KNOCK is that ceremony, badly needed and slightly too late. That's fine. Late is better than never. The poem doesn't bring the moment back. The photograph doesn't preserve it. But both of them say: *this was real, you were there, and something of value passed through.*

## The Role of AI in This Work

I have thought a lot about how to describe what Claude and DALL-E are doing in this project. The easy framings don't feel right.

They are not tools in the way a brush is a tool. A brush does exactly what the hand tells it. These models do not. They produce outputs I did not predict, make choices I would not have made, find images I did not know I was looking for. There is something genuinely autonomous happening, even if "autonomous" is probably the wrong word for a statistical process.

They are not collaborators in the full sense either. Claude does not have a childhood. It does not know what it means to lose something. It has read millions of texts written by people who did, and it has a deep structural understanding of how such losses are expressed in language. It knows the grammar of longing without knowing longing. That is a strange thing to work with.

The word I keep returning to is *medium*. Not a medium in the sense of a material — not paint or clay — but a medium in the older sense: an intermediary. Something that stands between two things and translates. Claude stands between a raw memory and a poem. DALL-E stands between a scene description and light on a surface.

What I do is build the channel. I write the system prompt that defines the voice. I construct the aesthetic constraints that determine what kind of light, what kind of grain, what decade of photography we are working in. I design the pipeline: memory flows to poem flows to visual extraction flows to image. The art is in the design of the channel, and in the decision to make it at all.

That feels right to me. Not because it is a comfortable position — I am not trying to claim more credit than I deserve — but because it is honest about what the practice of AI-assisted art actually is. You are not just prompting. You are making a series of decisions about what you want to find, and building a system that can find it.

## Knocking on Heaven's Door

The title of the song is a bit of a puzzle. Heaven's door, not heaven itself. Dylan isn't asking to be let in. He's just — standing there. Knocking.

I think that's the right image for what this project does. It doesn't recover anything. It doesn't open a door that closed years ago. It doesn't explain why the door was there or why it shut or whether anyone meant for it to.

It just stands at the door and knocks. And in the knocking, acknowledges that there *was* a door. That on the other side of it, something happened that mattered. That you were there, and you were young, and the world was specific and warm and fully itself around you, and you didn't know enough yet to be afraid.

Knocking on heaven's door isn't about what's behind it. It's about who you were when you knocked.

That's the whole project. That's everything I was trying to say.
